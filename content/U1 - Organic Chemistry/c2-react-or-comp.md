# C2 - Reactions of Organic Compounds

## C2.1 - Types of Organic Reactions

### Brief Introduction

- By using combinations of organic reactions, scientists can convert simple molecules into large, complex molecules
- i.e. TAXOL&trade; synthesized through organic reactions from organic comp. found in needles / twigs of European yew tree
	- used to treat ovarian cancer
- chemical reactions also used to convert petrol and natural gas into large, complex molecules like plastic
- rearrangement of bonds in organic compounds are slow
	- require continuous input of energy
	- catalysts may be used to speed up reactions that would take days, months, or longer
	- nearly all reactions result in mixture of products
	- unwanted products must be sep. from wanted product

### Addition, Elimination, and Substitution Reactions

- **addition reaction:** reaction in which atoms are added to a C-C double or triple bond
	- undergone by alkenes and alkynes

#### Hydrolysis Reaction

- **hydrolysis reaction:** reaction where a molecule is broken apart by adding hydroxyl group from a water molecule to one side of a bond...
	- ... and the corresponding H atom to the other side of the bond
	- *hydrolysis* = "break apart using water"
	- reverse of condensation reaction
- the bonds can incl. ester or amide bonds

***General Formula:***

![G.F.](img/c2/c2.1-hydrolysis-gf.png)

- Reaction can be opposite of esterification reaction
- $\therefore$ esterification reaction is reversible

*Reversibility of esterification reactions:*

![Desc. above](img/c2/c2.1-rev-ester-react.png)

- Some reactant and some product will always be present after esterification reaction is carried out
- Reaction will go further in one direction by adjusting certain factors
	- i.e. concentration of reactants increases &rarr; reaction shifts right
		- yields more products
	- i.e. base used to catalyze hydrolysis reaction
		- conds. favour hydrolysis
- In bio. sys., condensation and hydrolysis reactions performed in several steps
	- Each set of steps catalyzed by diff. enzymes
	- That controls direction of reactions

### Oxidation and Reduction Reactions

#### Oxidation

- **oxidation:** reaction where carbon atom forms more bonds to oxygen (O) atoms
	- or fewer bonds to hydrogen (H) atoms
	- occurs when organic compound reacts w/ oxidizing agent
- ox. that forms double $\ce{C-O}$ bonds are also elimination react.
- i.e. alcohols can be oxidized to form aldehydes and ketones
- *Common Oxidizing Agents*
	- acidified potassium permanganate, $\ce{KMnO4}$
	- acidified potassium dichromate, $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$
	- ozone, $\ce{O3}$
- $\ce{[O]}$ used to *symbolize* oxidizing agent

*Examples of oxidation reactions:*

![Ox. of alcohol](img/c2/c2.1-ox-alcohol.png)

![Ox. of aldehyde](img/c2/c2.1-ox-aldehyde.png)

- Ox. eq. often left unbalanced
- To identify oxidation, count C-H and C-O bonds in reactant and product
- It is oxidation if there are more C-O bonds and fewer C-H bonds in product than reactant

#### Reduction

- **reduction:** reaction where a carbon atom forms fewer bonds to oxygen (O) atoms
	- or more bonds to hydrogen (H) atoms
	- occurs when organic compound reacts w/ reducing agent
- A $\ce{C=O}$ or $\ce{C=C}$ bond is reduced to single bond by reduction
	- DB &rarr; SB reactions are also addition reactions
- i.e. aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids can be reduced to form alcohols
- *Common reducing agents*
	- lithium aluminum hydride: $\ce{LiAlH4}$
	- hydrogen gas over platinum catalyst $\ce{H2/Pt}$
- $\ce{[H]}$ used to *symbolize* reducing agent

*Examples of reduction reactions:*

![Ex.](img/c2/c2.1-red-ex.png)

- red. eq. often left unbalanced
- To identify reduction, count C-H and C-O bonds in reactant and product
- If there are fewer C-O bonds and more C-H bonds in product than reactant &rarr; reduction

#### Combustion Reaction

- **combustion reaction:** reduction reaction where a compound reacts w/ oxygen
	- ... to produce oxides of elements that make up compound
	- also process used for cellular respiration (obtain energy)
- **complete combustion reaction:** reaction hydrocarbon reacts w/ excess oxygen to produce carbon dioxide, water vapour, and release energy
	- produces blue flame
	> If excess oxygen is present, *all hydrocarbons will burn completely to produce carbon dioxide and water vapour.*

***General Formula for Complete Combustion:***

$$
\ce{hydrocarbon + O2(g) -> CO2(g) + H2O(g) + energy}
$$

- **incomplete combustion reaction:** combustion reaction that occurs when insufficient oxygen present
	- all elements in fuel will not combine w/ oxygen to greatest extent possible
	- produces orange / yellow flame
- *Products*
	- carbon monoxide (deadly gas)
	- **soot:** tiny pieces of unburned carbon

***General Formula for Incomplete Combustion,*** *(may vary):*

$$
\ce{hydrocarbon + O2(g) -> C(s) + CO(g) + CO2(g) + H2O(g) + energy}
$$

- orange colour in flame is glow from soot particles in flame
- carbon monoxide binds to hemoglobin in blood 20x more tightly than oxygen
	- prevents hemoglobin from transporting oxygen
- *not possible* to balance incomplete combustion equations
	- too difficult to predict how far combustion will go
	- ... and what combination of products will be formed

## C2.2 - Polymer Equations

### Polymer Chemistry

- **polymer:** very long molecule made by linking together repeating units of monomers
	- think of: chaining together paper clips (monomers)
	- i.e. polyethylene terephthalate (PET) used to make soft drink bottles
- **monomer:** a small molecule, linked covalently to others of same / similar type to form a polymer
- **plastic:** synthetic polymer that can be heated and moulded into specific shapes and forms

#### Examples

- adhesives, chewing gum, and polystyrene also made from synthetic polymers
- name of synthetic polymer usually written w/ prefix *poly-* before name of monomer
	- common name often used instead of proper IUPAC name
	- i.e. polyethylene = polymer made from ethene
		- common name is ethylene
	- i.e. polyvinylchloride (PVC) = polymer made from chloroethene
		- used to make numerous items
		- from adhesives to auto parts
	- i.e. *plasticizer* to PVC makes it very flexible
		- used in items like hoses, inflatables, and wire insulation
	- i.e. polypropylene = polymer made from propene
		- used to make plastics for labs and medical facilities
		- can withstand high temps. needed for sterilization
- some synthetic polymers can also be used to make fabric
	- i.e. rayon, polyester, nylon
- natural polymers also used to make fabrics
	- i.e. cotton, linen, and wool

### Polymers and Industry

- Organic compounds that industries use to make polymers start out as *petroleum*
- **petroleum:** fossil fuel that contains mixture of hydrocarbons, such as alkanes and alkenes
- **petrochemical:** product derived from petroleum that is converted into plastics and other synthetic materials
	- various organic reactions used to...
	- conv. petroleum &rarr; petrochemicals

**Production of Ethene:**

- Ethene prod. at large scale through process called "cracking at plants"
- Ethane from petroleum refines is heated to 800 &deg;C and undergoes following cracking reaction:

$$
\ce{C2H6(g) ->[\ Pt\ ] CH2 = CH2(g) + H2(g)}
$$

- DB makes ethene much more reactive than ethane
- Petrochemical industry uses ethene to synthesize useful materials like...
	- ethylene glycol &mdash; used in antifreeze
	- polyethylene
	- polyvinyl chloride

#### The Manufacture of Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC)

1. Ethene first reacted w/ chlorine in addition reaction to produce 1,2-dichloroethane:

$$
\ce{C2H4(g) + Cl2(g) -> C2H4Cl2(g)}
$$

2. 1,2-dichloroethane product is then cracked at high temps. to produce chloroethene (vinyl chloride) and hydrogen chloride via elimination:

$$
\ce{C2H4Cl2(g) -> C2H3Cl(g) + HCl(g)}
$$

3. By-product of cracking reaction, HCl(g) reacted further w/ oxygen and more ethene to produce more 1,2-dichloroethane for cracking:

$$
\ce{2C2H4(g) + 4HCl(g) + O2(g) -> 2C2H4Cl2(g) + 2H2O(g)}
$$

4. Vinyl chloride reacted in addition polymerization to produce PVC:

![Step 4](img/c2/c2.2-pvc-prod-step-4.png)

#### Polymer Production: Risks and Solutions

- 1970s: Workers at a U.S. plastic plant developed liver cancer due to long-term exposure to vinyl chloride, a carcinogen.  
- Vinyl chloride exposure is now regulated, but trace amounts persist in the environment from PVC production emissions and wastewater.  
- PVC manufacturing and disposal also release **dioxins**
	- **dioxins:** highly toxic chlorinated hydrocarbons formed during production or burning.  
	- Regulations and industry efforts have reduced dioxin emissions; backyard garbage burning is now the largest human source.  
	- Burning plastics releases harmful compounds like dioxins.  
- Most plastics degrade very slowly—taking hundreds of years.  
- **Recycling** is a key solution: plastics (~7% of garbage mass) can be reused to make products like bottles, bags, lumber, furniture, and fleece fabric (from PET bottles).  
	- Recycling has costs due to transport, sorting, cleaning, and processing.  
	- The **three R’s**—reduce, re-use, recycle—are essential for managing polymer waste.  
- **Bioplastics** offer an additional solution; they degrade faster under environmental conditions.  
	- **Hydrobiodegradable bioplastics** come from natural polymers (starch, cellulose) but may include some petroleum-based material.  
	- **Oxobiodegradable bioplastics** are petroleum-based with added catalysts that speed up degradation.

### Natural Polymers

Found in almost every living system

#### Polysaccharides

- **polysaccharides:** polymers made of saccharides
- **saccharides:** sugar monomers
- i.e. cellulose
	- provides most of plant structure
	- monomer: glucose
	- wood, paper, cotton, flax all made of cellulose
- i.e. *starch:* energy storage unit in plants

##### Cellulose vs. Starch

- cellulose forms **beta linkages**
	- indigestible by humans
	- bond from C to O drawn upward
- starch forms **alpha linkages**
	- digestible by humans
	- bond from C to O drawn downward
- *linkages* differ by orientation
- enzymes in our bodies recognize and break down alpha linkages in starch
- no animal has enzymes to digest beta linkages
- some animals host bacteria that can digest beta linkages for them
	- i.e. cows and termites

##### Branched Chains

- Starch can also have branched chains, but cellulose can't
- **amylose:** straight-chain starch
- **amylopectin:** branched-chain starch
	- there is a branch after every 24-30 glucose monomers
- **glycogen:** 3rd glucose polymer; energy storage unit in animals
	- has alpha linkage, digestible by humans
	- glycogen has many more branches than starch
	- it branches every 8-12 glucose units
	- extensive branching creates many free ends that allow enzymes to act on glycogen polymer at many pts. simultaneously
		- increases rate at which glucose can be released from storage
